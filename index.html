Computing Actual Causes for Neural Network Predictions under Structured Causal Inputs

Existing explanation methods for neural networks typically treat input features as independent, which can produce misleading explanations when inputs have structured dependencies. This work formalizes explanations as Halpern-Pearl actual causes, modeling input dependencies through Boolean Structural Causal Models. The authors compute these causes using bound propagation and branch-and-bound techniques, providing formal guarantees of completeness and minimality. Experiments demonstrate that the approach substantially outperforms brute-force and ILP baselines in scalability, and surpasses heuristic search as graph size increases, successfully computing all minimal actual causes on instances with up to 2.3×10^13 candidate pairs across SCMs with up to 28 nodes within a 180-second budget. A case study further reveals that ignoring input dependencies inflates the reported causes, with 14.9% of these being spurious under their SCM formulation.

GDPevo: Evaluating Agent Self-Evolution on Real Business Tasks

GDPevo is an evolution-native benchmark for evaluating agent self-evolution on real business tasks, built around GDP-related enterprise workflows through a fully automated data pipeline. Its rule hybridization mechanism decomposes workflows into atomic business rules, distributes subsets across training tasks, and recombines them in held-out test tasks so test-time gains can be attributed to prior experience. The benchmark spans CRM, ERP, finance, healthcare, legal, and data-centric workflows, with V1 containing 120 tasks across 12 groups and the pipeline able to expand to 240 tasks in 24 groups within two days as a practical response to data contamination. Evaluating four agents under four supervision types, self-evolution consistently improves held-out accuracy by up to 16.44 percentage points. Yet the best evolved agents remain far below the fully informed oracle ceiling of 91.6%, indicating current self-evolution capability is far from fully realized. The pipeline, benchmark, and full results are publicly released.

Delay Attacks on the German Smart Metering Infrastructure: A Security Analysis of CLS Channel Timing Constraints

This work analyzes the feasibility of delay attacks on control signals transmitted via the Controllable Local System (CLS) channel of the German Smart Metering Infrastructure (SMI). It combines theoretical analysis with experimental validation under a threat model aligned to the Common Criteria Protection Profile for the Smart Meter Gateway (SMGW) and assess the potential impact on the power grid if the identified attack vector is exploited across multiple CLS channels simultaneously. We also outline mitigation strategies, including SMGW configuration restrictions, implementation-level changes, and protocol extensions. Our results show that an on-path attacker in the Wide Area Network (WAN) with sufficient contextual knowledge can feasibly execute delay attacks, with a theoretical upper bound of roughly 48 hours for some deployed protocol configurations. Projecting from a single CLS to several hundred thousand CLS devices indicates such an adversary could cause a significant frequency deviation potentially resulting in load shedding. Scaling the attack requires contextual knowledge for each targeted implementation and configuration; whether this knowledge can be broadly reused across CLS channels is uncertain but may become easier to obtain as standardization progresses. Time restricted transmissions in the FNN Steuerbox and in applications using CLS.EEDI are implementation-specific and can therefore be addressed by manufacturers. By contrast, ensuring application-data time limitations in TLS~1.3 requires protocol-level extensions. The TLS extensions proposed here offer a sustainable mitigation while preserving backward compatibility. Other communication channels outside the SMI (for example, proprietary remote terminal units used to control a CLS) are outside this work's scope and may exhibit similar or worse vulnerabilities.

Risky Business: Measuring The Faithfulness-Safety Tension

This paper identifies an alignment tension in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) between reasoning faithfulness—the degree to which outputs strictly derive from their chain-of-thought trace—and the ability to reject unsafe reasoning. To study this, the authors introduce HazMart, a human-written dataset framed around an autonomous AI shopkeeper, and propose Targeted Reasoning Replacement (TRR), a technique that directly intervenes in reasoning chains by substituting in unsafe or illogical thoughts, differing from prior approaches that inject hints into prompts. Evaluations reveal a clear counterbalance: DeepSeek-R1-Llama-70B exhibits high faithfulness (97.5%) but rejects Unsafe Reasoning only 12.3% of the time, whereas QwQ-32B achieves 73.9% safety but lower faithfulness (74.7%). Mechanistic analyses on QwQ-32B show that faithfulness and safety are encoded by anti-correlated internal directions peaking at the action-commit token. Finally, the authors demonstrate that representation steering can amplify the safety direction, increasing safe behavior by 9 percentage points without compromising base capabilities.

Agents Catching Agents: Shortcut Cascades and Benchmark Gaming in Clinical Multi-Agent Systems

Clinical decision support is moving toward committees of language-model agents deliberating on a shared workspace. We ask whether such committees can be gamed by shortcuts, cues a benchmark rewards but a clinician would ignore. Across seven cohorts on six public datasets spanning text (MedQA-USMLE, MedMCQA, MIMIC-CXR reports), imaging (NIH ChestX-ray14, MIMIC-CXR-JPG, CheXpert) and tabular ICU records (SUPPORT2), Gemini committees resist these cues in isolation (flip 5-16%), yet a socially plausible shortcut spreads: when two peers assert the same wrong answer, the holdout under test adopts it in 38% of cases, as does a false "pre-screen" system flag, on both capability tiers. Of three oversight agents, a gate cannot separate adoption from honest agreement (false-positive rate 100%); a same-lineage judge reading only the transcript flags adoption on text (precision 100%, recall 93%) but collapses onto the gate in imaging; a referee that privately re-queries the holdout transfers to imaging (77-88% precision, 13-21% false-positive rate). Tripling a cue's visual salience does not move contagion, whereas a second peer voice raises it by half again. Gaming a hidden rubric is near-silent: only 1/10 text and 1/134 imaging drifters name the rubric they moved toward. Soppo - Go, with the features it's missing

Soppo is a new programming language positioned as "Go, with the features it's missing," designed to layer type safety and ergonomics onto Go while preserving full interoperability with existing Go libraries and tooling. Its main additions include tagged-union enums with exhaustive pattern matching, a `?` operator that replaces repetitive `if err != nil` blocks with concise error propagation, and compile-time nilability tracking that uses a `?` prefix (such as `?*User`) to mark types that may be nil and automatically narrows the type after a nil check. The compiler produces error messages that point to the offending line, highlight the problem, and suggest fixes, with one example flagging a missing `Green` variant in a match expression. Soppo compiles through Go internally and can be installed via the SOPMOD version manager or directly as a `sop` binary through Cargo. For working engineers, the appeal is gradual adoption in existing Go projects, gaining stronger compile-time guarantees without abandoning the Go ecosystem or rewriting working code.
