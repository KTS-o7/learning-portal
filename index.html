Generic Methods

Go 1.27 introduces type parameters on concrete methods, addressing a limitation present since generics were added in Go 1.18. While the earlier generics proposal omitted method type parameters because generic interface methods are difficult to implement efficiently, the Go team concluded that concrete generic methods are still valuable for code organization even though they cannot help implement interfaces. This addition enables more expressive chaining patterns, such as calling methods like Map on a generic List type that return new collections of different element types. Generic methods must be instantiated before use, either explicitly or implicitly, and can be converted to functions via method expressions for those who prefer that calling style. Generic interface methods remain unsupported because ensuring interface values could dispatch to any possible instantiation would require generating code for every type argument, which is impractical under Go's instantiation approach.

Never Bad-Mouth Your Past Employers

When asked in interviews why they want to leave their current job, many candidates vent their frustrations and badmouth current or former employers. However, Arpit Bhayani advises against this practice, noting that expressing negative feelings in a professional context can lead to severe consequences, including reputational damage. The tech industry is small, and after roughly a decade of experience, almost everyone becomes a first- or second-degree connection, making professional conduct especially important. While technical skills remain crucial, the ability to collaborate well and handle challenging situations professionally is equally valued. As Bhayani puts it, nobody wants to work with a "genius jerk," meaning candidates should always speak about ex-employers thoughtfully and maintain their professional reputation throughout interviews.

The Best Engineering Lessons Happen During Outages

Arpit Bhayani, Principal Engineer II at Razorpay, argues that the best engineering lessons emerge during production outages, as these situations reveal practical, real-world insights about systems, coding blunders, and tuning parameters. He actively participates in incident calls even when not on-call, noting that observing how senior engineers resolve issues under pressure with calm composure has shaped him into a better engineer. Bhayani also makes it a habit to read old postmortem documents (RCAs) from his workplaces, and he advises early-career engineers not to shy away from on-call duties and RCA reading. He dissects 18 production outages from companies like GitHub, Atlassian, and Spotify on his YouTube playlist, including one GitHub incident caused by a primary key exceeding its maximum value, which illustrated techniques for running data migrations without downtime.

BLOG

You don't have to be smart if you can think clearly

A distinction is drawn between "smart" engineers who rely on rapid intuitive leaps and "strong" engineers who can think clearly and methodically even when problems aren't solved instantly. Smart engineers often struggle or panic when encountering problems they can't immediately solve, since their professional identity is tied to quick problem-solving. Strong engineers, by contrast, use deliberate processes to work through challenges, focusing on invariants—beliefs about the system known to be true—to identify which assumptions might be false. The comparison is made to Formula 1 cars: high top speed but unusable at normal speeds due to specialized tires and brakes. The author connects this skill to Keats' concept of "negative capability"—being comfortable in uncertainty without forcing premature conclusions. Engineers who can think clearly during uncertainty tend to be highly effective, regardless of their ability to make intuitive leaps.
Sean Goedecke
BLOG

When In Doubt, Code It Out

Prototypes are fast, deliberately low-quality experiments designed for learning and gathering information, not for production or strict best practices. They help engineers validate technical feasibility, estimate project effort more reliably, and discover whether people care about a proposed solution. A short demo can reveal interest or disinterest before weeks of investment. Building a prototype is also an effective way to learn system design, advanced algorithms, and unfamiliar ideas through hands-on work. Since most prototypes take no more than 200 lines and are meant to be discarded, their value comes from the understanding they create. Approaching uncertain ideas as experiments and coding them out can lead to more informed decisions.
Arpit Bhayani

PAPER

Profit based evaluation of machine learning for nitrogen recommendations in winter wheat

The paper evaluates machine learning for nitrogen recommendations in winter wheat by scoring advice directly on profit rather than prediction accuracy, using 892 measured yield response curves from two long-running UK experiments across a wide range of price scenarios. Standard UK nitrogen advice ignores prices, yet recent price swings shifted the most profitable rate by tens of kilograms per hectare, motivating a price-aware approach. On the test bench, machine learning models failed as predictors: no model recovered the best rate within farm tolerance, and benchmark noise showed none could, so at normal prices every model lost to the standard advice on profit. The gain instead came from a simple correction step applied after the model, which cut profit losses by a quarter, with the same frozen correction reducing losses by 43% at the second site without retraining. A hybrid of standard advice with a damped correction removed bias and trimmed rare large losses, leading the authors to conclude that machine learning is best used as a profit-scored correction to standard advice rather than as a replacement.

PAPER

Common Geodesics Do Not Guarantee Fisher Consistency of the Structured SVM: Minimal Counterexamples and a Tree-Metric Classification

The paper demonstrates that a previously established necessary condition for Fisher consistency of the structured support vector machine—that the task loss be a metric in which every output triple has a common geodesic point—is not sufficient for the canonical coordinate-wise argmax decoder. The authors construct minimal counterexamples, showing that a four-output unit star admits an optimal score vector whose maximizers are all strictly non-Bayes, with four outputs being the minimum among metrics satisfying the common-geodesic condition. They provide a complete classification of positively weighted tree metrics, proving that argmax consistency holds if and only if the tree is a path, with failures on branching trees confined to boundary distributions. For full-support counterexamples under the common-geodesic condition, five outputs are shown to be necessary and sufficient, with the complete bipartite graph K_{2,3} as the smallest member of an infinite K_{m,n} family. The three-dimensional Hamming cube also yields a full-support counterexample, and all optimality claims are verified via exact primal-dual certificates.
arXiv · cs.LG · 10h
PAPER

When Interference Graphs Evolve: Doubly Robust Estimation of Dynamic Peer Effects

The paper addresses the challenge of estimating peer effects when interaction graphs change over time. The author introduces a controlled contrast framework that distinguishes between pre-assignment network history, dynamic peer exposure, and post-assignment network change, indexing potential outcomes by own treatment, temporally aggregated peer exposure, and a post-assignment evolution summary. The proposed Dynamic Network Doubly Robust estimator, DynaNet-DR, combines a temporally factorized propensity with normalized augmentation. Under conditions including consistency, sequential exchangeability, positivity, and weak dependence, the canonical estimator is consistent when either the outcome regression or propensity estimator is consistent. The implementation incorporates representative-score prediction, fixed clipping, and finite-sample stabilization. Semi-synthetic benchmarks on fixed real temporal graph sequences demonstrate favorable estimation accuracy, while a MathOverflow study serves as an observational illustration.

PAPER

TraceBench: Controlled Evaluation of LLM Agents for Time-Series Root-Cause Attribution

TraceBench is a simulation-based framework for generating controlled root-cause attribution tasks that evaluate LLM agents on time-series data from physical dynamical systems. In each task, an agent receives simulated time-series observations and must determine whether a system parameter was altered, and if so, which one. The authors generated tasks from three interpretable mechanical systems and systematically evaluated four LLM agents under controlled experimental conditions. Their results show that agents benefit substantially from domain context and explore data primarily through numerical console output rather than visualizations. Agents also generally performed worse when required to produce a Python script mapping each sample to a predicted label than when submitting predictions directly. The authors released their datasets, agent trajectories, experimental results, and a leaderboard on the TraceBench website.

PAPER

You may implement this later: Cofunctors as partial implementations

Vincent Wang-Maścianica’s paper presents cofunctors, also called retrofunctors, as a model of partial implementations. Unlike ordinary functors, which assign every operation in a specification a concrete instantiation at the outset, cofunctors accept an additional argument representing a state-dependent family of choices that may be made later. This interpretation allows system construction to defer backend decisions such as data representations and algorithms. The author emphasizes that cofunctors themselves are not new, but argues that their conceptual and purposive reading as partial implementations offers a novel perspective. The work is classified under Programming Languages and Logic in Computer Science and was submitted to arXiv in August 2026.

CEO fired developers to make room for AI. Developers create open source AI CEO

OpenExecutive is an open-source AI system developed by SenteLabsAI that operates as a virtual executive team, featuring eight specialist agents covering strategy, finance, HR, legal, operations, marketing, product, and board communications, all responding through a single unified executive voice. The platform is built on Python 3.11 with FastAPI, uses Anthropic Claude models (Sonnet 4-6 as default and Opus 4-7 for deep reasoning), and relies on ChromaDB for vector storage and SQLite for episodic memory that persists across sessions. It incorporates MBA-level built-in knowledge alongside user-uploaded company documents for context-aware responses, with prompt caching designed to achieve up to 85% cache hit rates. Users can interact through multiple interfaces including a Next.js web UI, Slack, email, Telegram, Google Chat, Discord, and CLI, and the system supports deployment to Fly.io with separate dev and QA environments. The project has gained notable traction with 1.5k stars on GitHub and is licensed under Apache 2.0.

Tailcat – Like netcat, but over Tailscale’s data plane

Tailcat is a userspace CLI and Go library that functions like netcat but transmits data over Tailscale's WireGuard-encrypted data plane without requiring the Tailscale control plane or account. One endpoint runs as a server and generates a short connection token derived from its WireGuard public key, which is shared out of band with the client to establish an end-to-end encrypted tunnel. Connections bootstrap through a DERP relay and then attempt NAT traversal for direct peer-to-peer UDP when possible. The tool supports multiple modes, including piping stdin/stdout, forwarding local TCP ports, running an auth-free SSH server, pinging, SOCKS5 proxying, and acting as an exit node. Keys can be ephemeral by default or saved for persistent addresses, and servers can restrict access to specific client public keys with the --allow flag.

Show HN: We built open OpenRouter that turns usage into a better model

Experiential is an open source gateway and router for agent workflows that provides a single OpenAI-compatible API across hosted, bring-your-own-key, and local models. It allows users to control which users and agents can access specific models and set spending limits per use case. The platform can convert production traffic into a custom router or model optimized for quality, speed, and cost. The system supports collecting OpenTelemetry traces to build simulation-based routers and fine-tune open source models through Tinker. The repository is licensed under Apache-2.0 and has 475 stars with 50 forks.

Show HN: My Claude quota ran out in 10 minutes, so I made a tool to find out why

Kelviq built "tare," a Claude Code skill that diagnoses why users burn through their AI quota so quickly, after their own 10-minute outage revealed runaway background token usage. The tool installs via a single npx command and works by teaching Claude Code to analyze its own local session logs, so users can ask plain-English questions like "why did I hit my usage limit yesterday?" or "which of my projects is eating my quota?" All analysis runs locally with no network connections, and shareable summaries strip out prompts, file paths, and identifiers. The tool deduplicates Claude Code's verbose log format (which inflates totals by about 86%) and attributes costs to the context that tools caused, not just what they returned. It can identify patterns like hundreds of short parallel sessions indicating automation rather than human use, and produces HTML reports for browsing or spreadsheets for export.

AWS Acquires DuckLabs

AWS has announced its acquisition of DuckLabs, effective in early September, with the team remaining together in Amsterdam to continue work on DuckDB, DuckLake, Quack, and related open-source projects. DuckLabs, founded five years ago and bootstrapped, grew to over 30 employees and built DuckDB into a widely used analytical database with more than one million daily downloads. DuckDB, DuckLake, and Quack will remain free and open source under the MIT license, with the nonprofit DuckDB Foundation continuing to steward them. The DuckLabs and AWS teams had collaborated for over a year prior to the acquisition, and AWS has committed to supporting DuckDB's continued development and community long term. Looking ahead, the DuckDB Foundation will add a technical advisory board, and DuckLabs plans to open the extension stack so extensions signed by other developers can run in DuckDB.
